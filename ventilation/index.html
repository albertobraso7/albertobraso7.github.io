01 / THE DESIGN QUESTIONS

Openings alone do not tell you which rooms will ventilate.

The architectural team needed to compare the existing and modified floor plans before finalizing the design. Flow distribution and solar exposure were complementary parts of that decision.

From supplied CAD to a useful comparison

The workflow began with geometry cleanup and solidification, followed by an external domain connecting the wind field to the interior through the actual door and window openings.

Airflow was studied in OpenFOAM, with selected Fluent cross-checks. Solar heat-flux exposure was analyzed separately in Fluent on the building envelope. Both work streams informed the same design comparison.

Choose scenarios that reveal sensitivity

The continuous wind problem was reduced to two opposing directions and three representative speeds. Applying that matrix to both floor plans exposes whether a design depends on one favorable wind condition.

The post-processing planes were selected around usable openings: one plan view and vertical sections through the entrance, bedroom doors and front window.

02 / THE ROOM-LEVEL COMPARISON

A new floor plan redirects the main flow path.

Wind enters as a concentrated jet and travels between openings. Rooms away from that pressure-driven path develop slower, recirculating flow.

Read a local trade-off

The modified layout changes which rooms lie on the main through-flow path. It redistributes ventilation instead of increasing velocity uniformly everywhere.

This matters to a design team because “more open area” is not a complete prescription: the position and connection of those openings determine where the momentum goes.

Reversed wind is a different problem

Internal partitions are not symmetric. Reversing wind direction changes the recirculation pattern rather than simply mirroring the original result.

The useful design comparison therefore asks which rooms are prioritized under each direction. A single global “better design” number would hide that decision.

03 / THE MECHANISM BEHIND THE PLAN VIEW

Use section cuts to explain why a flow pattern forms.

The plan view shows where air moves. Vertical sections add the mechanism: an entrance jet, roof-edge separation and the interaction between the external flow and the interior.

  1. External pressure fieldThe building redirects the approaching wind.
  2. Opening jetA concentrated flow enters through the windward opening.
  3. Interior distributionPartitions and connected openings steer the room-to-room path.
  4. RecirculationRegions away from that path exchange air differently.

The correspondence between horizontal and vertical cuts is also a quality check: the interpretation should agree across views, rather than depend on one convenient slice.

04 / BEYOND A SINGLE BUILDING

The neighboring house changes the incoming flow.

A companion study examined two houses under candidate site orientations. It extends the geometry question from an individual floor plan to the surrounding exposure.

Include the relevant surroundings

A nearby building changes the flow approaching an opening. Wake sheltering and channeling mean that an isolated-building assumption may miss the exposure relevant to the actual site.

The paired-house study compared orientation options and inspected the flow around and between both buildings. It demonstrates the need to choose the computational domain around the engineering question.

The available record supports the comparison method and visual interpretation, but not a recovered final winning orientation.

05 / SOLAR HEAT-FLUX EXPOSURE

The same massing that redirects air also casts shade.

The thermal work stream examined direct solar heat flux on the building envelope, adding a second physical consequence to the architectural geometry comparison.

Interpret exposure and self-shading

Sun-facing roof surfaces carry high direct flux while shaded walls are near the low end of the scale. Roof steps, parapets and the modified massing alter which surfaces shade one another.

The original images show heat-flux scales of roughly 1.0–1.1 kW/m². Those are surface-exposure levels under the plotted solar scenarios, not whole-building energy consumption.

Bring the two work streams back to design

Changing the openings and roof form affects both ventilation paths and solar exposure. The comparison gives the architectural team a physical basis for discussing the competing effects.

It does not turn the two analyses into a verified annual comfort or energy model. Their value here is a design-stage explanation of flow distribution and envelope exposure.

Engineering delivery: turn geometry changes into understandable consequences—where the air goes, why recirculation appears, and which surfaces the building shades.
