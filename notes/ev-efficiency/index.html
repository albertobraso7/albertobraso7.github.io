CHAPTER 01

Energy per distance

In brief

  • Energy per unit distance has the units of force. A steady force of 1 N costs 1 kJ per kilometre.
  • 1 N of road load = 0.278 Wh/km at the wheels. Divide by drivetrain efficiency to get battery energy.
  • Auxiliary loads are paid per unit of time. Their cost per kilometre is P/v, so they matter most at low speed.

Range is usable battery energy divided by energy consumed per distance. The second quantity is where engineering lives. If a vehicle needs a force F at the wheels to keep moving, the work done over a distance s is F·s. Energy per distance is therefore a force, and the familiar unit Wh/km is a force in disguise:

1 Wh/km = 3600 J / 1000 m = 3.6 N   ⇔   1 N = 0.278 Wh/kmAt the wheels. Battery-side consumption adds drivetrain losses and auxiliary loads.

This identity is the bridge between a simulation and a vehicle attribute. A drag force from external aerodynamics, a rolling-resistance force from a tyre model, or the force-equivalent of a pump's electrical power can all be expressed in Wh/km and then in kilometres or miles of range.

cbattery = Fwheel / ηdrivetrain + Paux / vSteady driving. c in J/m (multiply by 0.278 for Wh/km), v in m/s.

The two terms behave differently. The first is a property of the vehicle and its speed. The second is a property of time: a 1 kW heater costs 9 Wh/km at 113 km/h but 33 Wh/km at 30 km/h. Slow traffic and cold weather expose every watt of auxiliary power.

CHAPTER 02

Road load and the coast-down test

In brief

  • Road load = aerodynamic drag + rolling resistance + grade + inertia.
  • Drag force grows with v², drag power with v³. Above roughly 90 km/h a low-drag car spends most of its wheel energy on air.
  • Industry measures road load by coasting and fitting F = A + Bv + Cv². C is mostly aerodynamics; A is mostly tyres.
Froad = ½ ρ Cd A v² + Crr m g cos θ + m g sin θ + meff dv/dtmeff ≈ 1.03–1.05 m accounts for rotating wheels, rotor and gears. Pwheel = Froad · v.

Aerodynamic drag comes mainly from the pressure difference between the stagnation region at the front and the separated, low-pressure wake behind the vehicle. Rolling resistance is hysteresis: the tyre deforms at the contact patch and the rubber does not return all of that energy. Its coefficient Crr is typically 0.006–0.010 for passenger tyres and depends on compound, inflation pressure, load and temperature. Grade lifts the vehicle; most of that energy returns downhill through regenerative braking. Inertia accelerates it; part of that energy also returns through regeneration.

Where aerodynamics overtakes the tyres

v* = √( 2 Crr m g / (ρ Cd A) )For Cd 0.20, A 2.3 m², 2,400 kg and Crr 0.008: v* ≈ 26 m/s ≈ 94 km/h ≈ 58 mph.

The coast-down test

On a test track the vehicle is brought to high speed, shifted to neutral and allowed to decelerate. Because meff·dv/dt = −F(v), the measured deceleration gives road load directly. Fitting a quadratic gives the road-load coefficients:

F(v) = A + B v + C v²A ≈ rolling resistance and constant drag (brakes, bearings). B ≈ speed-dependent tyre and driveline losses. C ≈ ½ ρ Cd A plus any speed-squared tyre effects.

These coefficients are loaded onto the chassis dynamometer, so every laboratory drive-cycle test reproduces the road. SAE J1263 and J2263 describe the procedures; J2263 adds on-board anemometry to account for wind. Runs are made in both directions to cancel wind and slope.

CHAPTER 03

Drive cycles and the range label

In brief

  • Official consumption comes from speed-versus-time schedules driven on a dynamometer. The urban (UDDS) and highway (HWFET) schedules below are the actual EPA traces.
  • A backward-facing model follows the trace: speed → wheel force → drivetrain efficiency → battery power, with regeneration when braking.
  • In the city, inertia and regeneration dominate. On the highway, aerodynamics dominates.

The US label combines city and highway results with a 55/45 weighting. Manufacturers can run the two-cycle test and multiply the result by 0.7 to approximate real-world driving, or run the full five-cycle procedure, which adds an aggressive high-speed cycle (US06), a hot cycle with air conditioning (SC03) and a cold-temperature city cycle. Battery electric vehicles follow the SAE J1634 multi-cycle range test. The simulator below is a simplified model of the physics, not the certification procedure.

Backward-facing and forward-facing models

A backward-facing model starts from the required speed trace and computes what each component must deliver, working back from the wheels to the battery. It is fast, always follows the cycle and suits efficiency and range studies. A forward-facing model starts from a driver controller that commands torque; components respond with their own dynamics and limits, and the vehicle speed is an output. It is slower but captures controls, saturation and transients, which matters for performance, thermal transients and controller development. Commercial system tools such as GT-SUITE, Simulink/Simscape and Simcenter Amesim support both approaches.

CHAPTER 04

Aerodynamic drag

In brief

  • Range responds to the drag area CdA, not to Cd alone.
  • Pressure drag dominates passenger cars; the rear end and wake are the main levers, followed by wheels, underbody and cooling flow.
  • CFD predicts differences between variants more reliably than absolute drag, unless the test conditions and the wake are well resolved.

A drag count is 0.001 in Cd. On a low-drag sedan at highway speed, aerodynamics is a little more than half of battery energy, so 10 counts (5% of a Cd of 0.20) change consumption by roughly 3%. Use the calculator in chapter 2 to see the value for your own vehicle and speed.

Where the drag comes from

  • Rear end and wake. A tapered roof and sides with a sharp cut-off edge give a smaller, steadier wake and higher base pressure.
  • Wheels and wheelhouses. Rotating, open and turbulent; often a fifth to a third of total drag. Wheel covers, tyre profile, deflectors and air curtains are the levers.
  • Underbody. A flat, closed floor and a rear diffuser. The battery pack of an electric vehicle naturally provides a smooth floor.
  • Cooling flow through the heat exchangers (chapter 5), and protuberances such as mirrors and handles.
  • Ride height. Lowering at speed reduces frontal area and underbody flow.

Yaw and wind-averaged drag

Natural wind combined with driving speed gives a yaw angle of a few degrees, and drag usually rises with yaw. Wind-averaged drag evaluates Cd at several yaw angles and weights them by how often they occur, so a design that only wins at zero yaw does not look better than it is.

CHAPTER 05

Cooling drag and front-end airflow

In brief

  • Air that passes through the heat exchangers loses momentum; that momentum deficit is drag.
  • More cooling air means more heat rejection and more drag. Active shutters let the thermal system take only the air it needs.
  • The cooling demand peaks when ram air is weakest: fast charging and slow, high-load driving.
  • Cooling drag costs energy per kilometre in proportion to V², a fan at constant power in proportion to 1/V. At low speed the shutters open and the fan works; at high speed the shutters close as far as the heat load allows.
Dcooling ≈ ṁair (V∞ − Vexit)The momentum lost by the cooling air between the grille and its exit into the underbody or wheel arches.

Electric vehicles need less cooling air at cruise than combustion vehicles, but they still reject heat from the air-conditioning condenser or heat-pump outdoor exchanger, the power electronics and the battery during fast charging. Sealing the front end so that no air bypasses the cores, and guiding the exit flow cleanly, are classic gains. The fan is a pure auxiliary load, often several hundred watts at full speed.

In CFD, each core is usually a porous medium whose resistance is fitted to the measured pressure drop against face velocity, with the fan represented by a pressure jump from its curve or by a rotating zone when swirl matters:

Δp / L = (μ / α) v + C2 · ½ ρ v²Darcy–Forchheimer form: a viscous term and an inertial term.

The cooling-air operating point

Air through the front end is pushed by ram pressure and the fan, and resisted by the grille, shutters, heat-exchanger cores, underhood components and the exit. The flow settles where the pressure available equals the losses, exactly like a pump on its system curve (chapter 8). The heat the cores can reject then follows from the air and coolant capacity rates.

What to look for. At highway speed, ram pressure supplies more air than the heat load needs, so the shutters can close until the rejection just meets the demand; the drag saved is free. Below about 50 km/h ram air alone no longer meets the demand, and at standstill only the fan moves air: that is why hot-day fast charging sizes the fan and the condenser. Heat rejection grows more slowly than air flow because the effectiveness falls as the air capacity rate rises.

Two ways to pay for cooling air

Air reaches the heat exchangers in two ways, and each has its own price. Ram air is free to move but costs drag, paid at the wheels. The fan costs electrical power directly. The two prices scale in opposite directions with speed, which is why the right answer in the city is the wrong one on the highway.

Choosing the cheapest actuator

The thermal controller does not ask for a shutter position or a fan speed. It asks for heat rejection: a coolant temperature, or a refrigerant high-side pressure, held at its target by a PI loop like the one in chapter 16. A second layer, the arbitration, decides which actuator delivers that rejection at the lowest total power:

  • Inputs: coolant temperatures in each loop, refrigerant high-side pressure, vehicle speed, ambient temperature, cabin demand and charging state.
  • Objective: minimize drag power plus fan power plus pump power, subject to every temperature and pressure staying on target.
  • Usual sequencing: open the shutters first, then start the fan. Running the fan against partly closed shutters wastes fan power on the shutter loss.
  • Practical rules: hysteresis or rate limits so the actuators do not hunt; discrete fan steps and a noise limit at low speed; shutters fail open; frost detection; a position calibration at start-up.

PUBLIC EXAMPLE · MANUFACTURER DATA

Lucid describes a patented vortex-generating intake behind the front bumper of the Lucid Air. It distributes air uniformly over the heat-exchange surfaces while keeping the intake aperture small. The engineering logic: a core with uniform face velocity transfers more heat per unit of area than one with maldistributed flow (the same effect as uneven flow between cold plates, chapter 7), so the same heat rejection needs less air and less cooling drag. Lucid reported a drag coefficient of 0.21 for the Air in 2020. Source: Lucid media room, accessed November 2026.

Check yourself
  1. Why do shutters save more on the highway than in the city? Cooling drag power grows with V³ and its energy per kilometre with V². The same opening costs about 36 times more per kilometre at 120 km/h than at 20 km/h, (120/20)².
  2. When does the front end need the most air? High thermal load at low speed: hot-day fast charging at standstill, city traffic with air conditioning on a hot day, a long grade or towing. Those cases size the fan and the condenser.
  3. Why not run the fan on the highway instead of opening the shutters? Ram pressure at highway speed is already larger than the fan's shut-off pressure, so the fan adds little flow. At speed the only way to get more air is to open the shutters.
  4. How would you control the shutters and the fan? A PI loop asks for heat rejection, on coolant temperature or refrigerant pressure. An arbitration layer delivers it at the lowest drag plus fan power: shutters first, then the fan, with hysteresis, fail-open shutters and frost detection.
  5. Why do shutters matter in winter? Closed, they speed up warm-up and cut drag. With a heat pump, the front exchanger becomes the evaporator and needs air again, so the shutters open for heating demand and must handle frost.

WHERE CFD FITS

A front-end airflow model gives radiator airflow against vehicle speed, with the fan on and off, as the boundary condition for the 1D thermal model. The same model gives the drag cost of each shutter position, so heat rejection and drag can be traded in one place.

CHAPTER 06

Drive-unit losses

In brief

  • Copper loss grows with torque squared; iron loss grows with speed. Peak efficiency is high, but cruising sits at low torque where iron loss is a large share.
  • Copper resistance rises about 0.39% per kelvin, and permanent magnets lose flux as they heat. Cooling is an efficiency topic as well as a durability one.
  • The inverter is limited by its junction temperature. The thermal path from die to coolant sets how much current the silicon can carry, and the temperature swing in every acceleration sets its life.
  • The number that matters is efficiency weighted over the drive cycle, not the peak.

Light load and the inverter

The inverter turns battery DC into three-phase AC by switching each phase between the DC rails thousands of times per second. Its two loss mechanisms behave differently. Conduction loss is the voltage drop across the conducting device times the current. Switching loss is the energy dissipated in every turn-on and turn-off, paid at each switching event.

MOSFET: Pcond = 3 Irms² Rds,on(Tj)    IGBT: Pcond ≈ 3 (V0 Ī + r Irms²)    Psw ≈ 3 fsw Esw (Vdc / Vref)(Ī / Iref)Ī = 2√2 Irms / π is the mean absolute phase current. A silicon IGBT has a knee voltage V0 of roughly 0.8–1 V that it pays at any current; a silicon-carbide MOSFET behaves like a resistor, so its conduction loss falls with current squared.

Cruising needs a small fraction of rated current, and that is where a vehicle covers most of its distance. There the IGBT's knee voltage and higher switching energy dominate, while the MOSFET's losses nearly vanish. Silicon carbide's wide bandgap and roughly ten times higher critical field allow a thin, low-resistance drift layer at 1,200 V, which is why it is common in 800 V drives. Its on-resistance rises with junction temperature, so inverter cooling is also an efficiency lever.

Cooling the inverter: from junction to coolant

The inverter's losses are generated in a few square centimetres of semiconductor die. What limits the inverter is the junction temperature, not the coolant: about 175 °C for silicon-carbide MOSFETs and 150–175 °C for silicon IGBTs. Heat leaves the die through the die attach, a ceramic substrate with copper on both sides, a baseplate and the coolant channels of a cold plate. Each layer adds a thermal resistance and a heat capacity. A pulse of a fraction of a second therefore heats mainly the die, while a pulse of several seconds reaches the coolant.

Tj(t) = Tcoolant + P · Zth(t)    Zth(t) = Σ Ri (1 − e−t/τi)Zth is the transient thermal impedance, measured by the module maker and fitted with a Foster network: a sum of RC terms. The fit reproduces the curve but its terms are not the physical layers; a Cauer network, a ladder of the real layers, is needed when another layer such as a different cold plate is added.
  • Indirect cooling: a flat baseplate bolted to a cold plate with thermal grease between them. The grease layer is often the largest single resistance in the path.
  • Direct cooling: a baseplate with pin fins sits in the coolant itself, or the channels are cast into the housing that also carries the motor. There is no grease layer, and the wetted area is larger.
  • Efficiency: the on-resistance of a SiC MOSFET rises with temperature, about 0.6% per kelvin in the calculator above, so a cooler junction also loses less. A lower thermal resistance lets the same die carry more current, or lets a design use less silicon, which is the most expensive part of the inverter.
  • Life: every acceleration heats and cools the junction, and the swing ΔTj fatigues bond wires and solder layers. Power-cycling life follows roughly Nf ∝ ΔTj−5, a Coffin–Manson relation, so a 15% smaller swing is worth about twice the life.
  • Place in the loop: the inverter is often first in the coolant loop it shares with the motor's oil-to-coolant heat exchanger, because its junction limit leaves the least margin above the coolant temperature.

Temperature limits: copper, insulation and magnets

  • Copper. Resistance rises 0.39% per kelvin, so a hot winding loses more for the same torque (equation above).
  • Insulation. Thermal ageing follows an Arrhenius law; a common rule of thumb is that insulation life halves for every 8–10 K above its rating. The insulation class sets a limit on the hottest point of the winding (155 °C for class F, 180 °C for class H), not on its average.
  • Magnets. NdFeB remanence falls by about 0.1% per kelvin, so a hot motor needs more current for the same torque, which raises copper loss further. High temperature combined with a strong demagnetizing current can cause irreversible loss, so magnet temperature is a limit as well.

Peak torque is limited by current and by how long the winding takes to reach its limit. Continuous torque is limited by the steady balance between losses and the thermal resistance from the hot spot to the coolant.

Two motors: torque split as an efficiency control

A dual-motor vehicle can share the requested torque between its axles freely, within traction limits. Copper loss grows with torque squared, so an even split halves it. Battery and voltage

In brief

  • Range uses usable energy; a buffer at both ends of the state of charge protects cell life.
  • Internal loss is I²Rint. Resistance rises sharply in the cold.
  • Doubling pack voltage halves current for the same power and divides resistive losses by four in the same conductors.
  • A cell is modelled as an open-circuit voltage plus a resistance and RC pairs, fitted from a pulse test. How fast the test is logged changes what the test shows.

Cells prefer a narrow temperature window. Cold cells have high resistance and risk lithium plating while charging; hot cells age faster. Uniformity matters as much as the mean: cells at different temperatures age at different rates, so a cold plate must remove heat evenly, which makes battery cooling a flow-distribution problem.

P = V I  →  Ploss = I² R = (P / V)² R250 kW at 800 V is 312 A; at 400 V it is 625 A and four times the resistive heat in the same conductors.

A dimpled or ribbed cold plate can be reduced the same way as any compact heat-exchanger surface: simulate one periodic cell of the pattern, extract the Fanning friction factor and the Colburn j factor against Reynolds number, and use those correlations in a plate-level or pack-level model. The trade-off is explicit: more heat transfer costs pressure drop, and pressure drop costs pump power (chapter 8).

Inside one cell: the equivalent-circuit model

System models rarely solve the electrochemistry. They use an equivalent circuit: the open-circuit voltage U, which depends on the state of charge and temperature, in series with an ohmic resistance R0 and one or two RC pairs. R0 (electrolyte, current collectors, contacts) responds instantly. A fast RC pair, about a second, stands for charge transfer at the electrodes; a slow pair, tens of seconds, for diffusion of lithium in the active material. The parameters come from a pulse test (HPPC, hybrid pulse power characterization): a current step from rest, held for about 10 s, then rest again.

V = U(SOC, T) − I R0 − V1 − V2    dVk/dt = I / Ck − Vk / (Rk Ck)    Q̇ = I (U − V) − I T ∂U/∂TI positive in discharge; τk = Rk Ck. The heat expression (Bernardi) has an irreversible part, ohmic and polarization losses that grow with I², and a reversible, entropic part proportional to I whose sign flips between charge and discharge.

The same model runs inside the battery management system. An extended Kalman filter compares the measured terminal voltage with the model's prediction to estimate the state of charge: a virtual sensor for a quantity no instrument measures directly.

The hottest cell decides

A battery is limited by its worst cell. Charging current, power and ageing are set by the hottest cell (or, in winter, the coldest), so a cooling design is judged by the maximum and the spread, not the mean. Wound cells are strongly anisotropic: heat flows easily along the metal current collectors and poorly across the layers of electrode and separator.

Plates in series all carry the full flow, so their heat-transfer coefficient is high, but the coolant warms along the path and the pressure drops add up. Plates in parallel all see the inlet temperature and the loop pressure drop is low, but each plate gets only part of the flow, and the split depends on the manifold. Operating point, viscosity and cavitation

The pump only sees the loop through its system curve, H = kQ², where k sums every restriction: valves, cold plates, chiller, motor jacket, hoses. Removing a restriction lowers k, so the same flow needs less head and less power, or a smaller pump. Cold coolant raises viscosity, which raises k and degrades the pump's own curve. If inlet pressure approaches vapour pressure (hot coolant, restrictive suction, low system pressure), cavitation reduces head and erodes the impeller; suction-side restriction matters more than discharge-side.

Fans and compressors

Radiator fans obey the same affinity laws, so two fans at moderate speed can cost less than one at full speed. The refrigerant compressor is the largest thermal auxiliary load. Its power rises with pressure ratio, so pressure losses on the refrigerant side, in lines, chiller, condenser and valves, raise compressor power and lower the system coefficient of performance.

CHAPTER 10

Fast-charging heat

In brief

  • Charging power tapers as cell voltage approaches its limit; 10–80% time is the usual metric.
  • Heat generation scales with current squared, and the vehicle is standing still: the chiller and the front-end fan carry the entire load.
  • Better heat-exchanger models let a design charge closer to the cell limit without oversizing the cooling.
  • Charging power is V × I. A higher pack voltage carries the same power with less current, so the connector limit binds later and the conductors heat less; the heat inside the same cells does not change.
Qheat ≈ (1 − ηcharge) PchargeA 3% loss at 250 kW is 7.5 kW of heat in cells, busbars, cables and power electronics.

The limits are the charger, the connector current, the cell voltage and temperature. Cold cells risk lithium plating and must be heated first; hot cells are derated to protect life. The chiller, a refrigerant-to-coolant heat exchanger, links the battery loop to the refrigerant cycle, so its pressure drop and conductance enter both the coolant pump and the compressor power.

Who limits the charge

P = min( Pcells(SOC, T),  Pstation,  Vpack Imax )Imax is the lower of the station's and the connector's current rating. At 500 A a 400 V-class pack takes less than 200 kW; an 800 V-class pack can take about twice that before the current limit applies.

It tapers at high state of charge, where the anode potential approaches the plating threshold, and falls in the cold, where slower kinetics make plating easier. Which of the three limits is active changes during a session, and a good vehicle model reports it.

Why warmer oil can save energy

Colder oil cools better, so why ever run it warmer? Because a transmission fluid's viscosity falls several-fold between 40 and 100 °C, which reduces gear churning, bearing drag and pumping work. At light load copper loss is small, so warm oil wins. At high load I²R dominates and grows with temperature, and the winding limit is close, so cold oil wins. A valve or bypass around the oil-to-coolant heat exchanger moves the operating point between the two: a constrained optimization that a plant model can solve and a controller can schedule.

Architecture decisions before prototypes

Most architecture choices are made before hardware exists, and a system model is how they are made. Size on the worst cases (hot-day fast charge, sustained grade, cold-soak start), then compare energy on the common ones (certification cycles and typical commutes). A layout that wins the sizing case can still lose the energy case.

Tj ≈ Tcoolant + Ploss ΣRthSteady junction temperature of a power semiconductor: coolant temperature plus loss times the thermal resistance from junction to coolant.

The order of components in a series coolant loop follows from this. Power electronics have the lowest temperature limit and the smallest thermal mass, so they usually take the coldest coolant first; the motor's oil-to-coolant heat exchanger accepts the warmer coolant downstream. Parallel branches give every component the same inlet temperature and a lower loop pressure drop, at the cost of a flow split that must hold across temperature and flow rate, which is a valve and manifold design problem (chapter 13).

Fast and slow dynamics

A semiconductor junction reaches most of its steady temperature rise within seconds, a winding within minutes, a battery pack over tens of minutes. A full-power launch is therefore a thermal event for the inverter but not for the battery. The inverter needs fast, model-based junction-temperature estimation and derating; the battery can be managed predictively, for example by preconditioning on the way to a charger. CHAPTER 14

One language for heat, flow and charge

In brief

  • Heat, fluid and electric charge all move down the gradient of a potential: temperature, pressure and voltage. In each case, flow = potential difference / resistance.
  • The same series and parallel rules size a battery pack, a coolant loop and the thermal path of a motor winding.
  • The analogy is exact for linear laws (conduction, laminar flow, Ohm's law). Turbulent pressure drop, convection and radiation make the resistance depend on the operating point, which is where CFD earns its place.

A vehicle thermal and energy model couples electrical, hydraulic and thermal networks. They are easier to build, debug and explain once one notices that they are the same mathematics with different names.

Where the analogy bends

  • Turbulent flow. Pressure drop grows as Δp = k Q², so the "resistance" Δp / Q = k Q rises with flow. A system model uses the local slope dΔp/dQ = 2kQ, or the full curve from CFD or test (chapter 13).
  • Convection. h grows roughly as Re0.8 in turbulent flow, so the convective resistance falls as flow rises, while conduction resistances do not change. That is why doubling coolant flow never doubles heat transfer.
  • Radiation. Q̇ = ε σ A (T1⁴ − T2⁴): strongly non-linear; it is linearized as hrad ≈ 4 ε σ T³ around the operating point.
  • Properties that move. Copper resistance rises 0.39%/K, oil viscosity falls several-fold with temperature (chapter 11), and cell resistance rises in the cold (chapter 10).
  • No thermal inertia. Electrical circuits have inductance and fluid lines have inertance (ρL/A, the cause of water hammer), but heat has no inertia. A thermal network is resistances and capacitances only, so on its own it never overshoots; oscillation in a thermal system comes from its control loop.

The Reynolds analogy: why more heat transfer costs pressure drop

Turbulent eddies carry momentum and heat together, so friction and heat transfer rise together. For smooth turbulent duct flow the Colburn analogy links them directly:

j = St · Pr2/3 ≈ f / 2St = h / (ρ cp u), Stanton number; f, Fanning friction factor. A surface whose j/f beats the smooth-duct value, such as dimples, louvres or offset fins, buys heat transfer at a lower pressure cost. Compact heat-exchanger design is largely the search for a high j/f.

Time constants everywhere

Every network that stores something has a time constant τ = R·C. Electrically it is the RC of a filter or of a cell's diffusion branch. Thermally it is Rth·m cp: seconds for a semiconductor junction, minutes for a winding (chapter 6), tens of minutes for a battery pack (chapter 11). The equivalent-circuit battery model is this analogy used in reverse: an ohmic resistance plus RC pairs that imitate charge transfer and diffusion.

Check yourself
  1. What drives heat, fluid, current and lithium ions? Gradients of temperature, pressure, voltage and electrochemical potential.
  2. What is the thermal equivalent of a capacitor, and of an inductor? Thermal mass, m cp. There is no thermal inductor: heat has no inertia.
  3. Two identical cold plates carry a total flow Q. Series or parallel: what happens to the pressure drop? In series each plate sees Q and the drops add: 2kQ². In parallel each sees Q/2 and the loop drop is k(Q/2)² = kQ²/4, eight times lower, but each plate has half the flow and a lower h.
  4. Why does doubling the coolant flow not double heat transfer? Only the convective resistance falls, and only by about 20.8; conduction resistances in series stay the same.
  5. A winding's heat path is copper → insulation → oil film → oil. Which resistance do you attack first? The largest one. In series the resistances add, so improving a small one changes little.
  6. The same cells rearranged from 400 V to 800 V: why is the heat inside the cells unchanged? Pack resistance rises four times while current halves: (I/2)² · 4R = I²R. The external conductors keep their resistance, so their heat falls to a quarter.

From a vehicle log to a correlated model

In brief

  • Vehicle signals travel as CAN messages. A DBC file says where each signal sits in a message and how to scale it to physical units.
  • Signals arrive at different rates and with different delays. Put them on one time base and align them before comparing with a model.
  • When a model and a test disagree, check the comparison first: energy boundary, inputs, timing, sensor offset and gain. Change the physics only after that.

From bytes to a signal

A CAN frame carries an identifier and up to 8 data bytes (64 in CAN FD). The DBC file is the network's dictionary: for each signal it gives the start bit, length, byte order, sign, scale factor, offset and unit, and the physical value is raw × factor + offset.

In practice python-can reads the log formats (BLF, ASC, candump) and cantools decodes them with the DBC; MATLAB's Vehicle Network Toolbox does the same.

Rates, delays and one time base

  • Different rates. A typical log might carry pack current at 100 Hz, pack voltage at 10 Hz, vehicle speed at 20 Hz and cabin signals at 1 Hz or only on change.
  • One time base. Interpolate continuous signals onto the fastest relevant rate. Hold the last value for states such as mode, gear or valve position: interpolating a gear number is meaningless.
  • Multiply, then average. Compute power V × I at the high rate before any averaging. When voltage sags with current, the mean of the product is not the product of the means.
  • Differentiate with care. Acceleration taken from a speed signal with 0.01 km/h resolution at 20 Hz carries steps of up to about 0.03 m/s², which become kilowatts of false inertial power at highway speed. Filter before differentiating, or use a wheel-speed or accelerometer signal.
  • Delays. Each control unit and gateway adds latency, tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Find a constant shift by cross-correlating two signals that should move together, such as logged and modelled power.

CHAPTER 16

A coupled model with a controller

In brief

  • A vehicle energy model couples mechanical, electrical and thermal blocks. Each block can be simple; the value is in the connections.
  • Thermal states carry the coupling in time: losses heat the machine, its temperature changes the losses, and a controller decides how much the cooling costs.
  • A controller is part of the efficiency: the same hardware uses less pump energy with a better strategy, and a virtual sensor gives it the temperatures no instrument measures.

This chapter connects models from earlier chapters into one system: the road-load equation (chapter 2) and an EPA speed trace (chapter 3) set the demand, the loss map of chapter 6 turns it into heat and electrical power, a battery with internal resistance (chapter 7) supplies it, and a three-node thermal network with a pump and radiator (chapters 8 and 14) carries the heat away. A controller sets the pump flow.

Causal and acausal, backward and forward

  • Causal (signal-flow) models fix every block's inputs and outputs. They suit controllers and quasi-static energy chains; the modeller decides the causality.
  • Acausal (physical-port) models connect components through pairs of variables, an across variable such as temperature, pressure or voltage and a through variable such as heat flow, volume flow or current, as in chapter 14. The tool assembles and orders the equations, so a component library can be reused in any circuit.
  • Backward-facing models start from the speed trace and ask what power it needs, as here. They are fast and suit energy and thermal studies. Forward-facing models add a driver controller that requests torque to follow the trace; they are needed when actuator limits, drivability or a controller's response matter.
  • Time scales span seconds for the vehicle, minutes for the machine and coolant, and hours for the battery's state of charge. Stiff systems call for implicit, variable-step solvers. Coupling between tools runs through the FMI standard (an FMU per model), and the same model travels from model-in-the-loop to software- and hardware-in-the-loop testing of the controller.

The controller and the virtual sensor

A thermostat switches the pump fully on above the set point plus half a hysteresis band and to minimum flow below it. A PI controller sets the flow continuously from the error and its integral, with anti-windup so the integral stops growing while the pump is saturated. Because pump power rises with the cube of flow (chapter 8), a steady partial flow removes the same heat for less energy than switching between minimum and full.

The winding temperature is the one that limits the machine, but it is rarely measured in a production vehicle. A virtual sensor estimates it. The naive version applies the steady-state network to the instantaneous losses; the working version runs the thermal network in real time, fed by the measured coolant temperature and the estimated losses, and a production version would also correct it with any available measurement (a Kalman filter, as for the battery in chapter 7).

The naive virtual sensor misses the winding temperature by up to about 80 K during transients; the observer, even with its 15% calibration error, stays within about 3 K.

Check yourself
  1. What is the difference between a causal and an acausal model? A causal model fixes each block's inputs and outputs, like Simulink signals. An acausal model connects components through physical ports carrying an across and a through variable, and the tool writes the conservation equations; Modelica, Amesim, GT-SUITE and Simscape work this way.
  2. Backward- or forward-facing: which do you need? Backward-facing for energy and thermal studies over a known trace: fast and simple. Forward-facing, with a driver model, when actuator limits, controller response or drivability matter.
  3. Loss depends on temperature and temperature on loss. How does the model solve that loop? Temperature is a state with thermal capacitance; the loss is evaluated at the current state and the state is integrated forward in time, so there is no algebraic loop.
  4. Why does a PI controller save pump energy compared with a thermostat holding the same temperature? Pump power scales with Q³. Holding a steady partial flow costs less than alternating between minimum and full flow for the same heat removal.
  5. How would you estimate a winding temperature nobody measures? Run a thermal network in real time from measured coolant temperature and estimated losses, calibrated on a dynamometer with thermocouples in the winding, and correct it with any measurement available. A steady-state formula ignores thermal mass and is wrong by tens of kelvin in transients.
  6. How would a model like this be validated? Component first: loss map on a dynamometer, thermal network against winding thermocouples, radiator and pump against bench curves. Then the assembled model against vehicle logs over a drive cycle (chapter 15), with agreed acceptance bands.
