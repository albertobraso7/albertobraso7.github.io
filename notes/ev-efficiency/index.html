CHAPTER 01

Energy per distance

In brief

  • Energy per unit distance has the units of force. A steady force of 1 N costs 1 kJ per kilometre.
  • 1 N of road load = 0.278 Wh/km at the wheels. Divide by drivetrain efficiency to get battery energy.
  • Auxiliary loads are paid per unit of time. Their cost per kilometre is P/v, so they matter most at low speed.

Range is usable battery energy divided by energy consumed per distance. The second quantity is where engineering lives. If a vehicle needs a force F at the wheels to keep moving, the work done over a distance s is F·s. Energy per distance is therefore a force, and the familiar unit Wh/km is a force in disguise:

1 Wh/km = 3600 J / 1000 m = 3.6 N   ⇔   1 N = 0.278 Wh/kmAt the wheels. Battery-side consumption adds drivetrain losses and auxiliary loads.

This identity is the bridge between a simulation and a vehicle attribute. A drag force from external aerodynamics, a rolling-resistance force from a tyre model, or the force-equivalent of a pump's electrical power can all be expressed in Wh/km and then in kilometres or miles of range.

cbattery = Fwheel / ηdrivetrain + Paux / vSteady driving. c in J/m (multiply by 0.278 for Wh/km), v in m/s.

The two terms behave differently. The first is a property of the vehicle and its speed. The second is a property of time: a 1 kW heater costs 9 Wh/km at 113 km/h but 33 Wh/km at 30 km/h. Slow traffic and cold weather expose every watt of auxiliary power.

CHAPTER 02

Road load and the coast-down test

In brief

  • Road load = aerodynamic drag + rolling resistance + grade + inertia.
  • Drag force grows with v², drag power with v³. Above roughly 90 km/h a low-drag car spends most of its wheel energy on air.
  • Industry measures road load by coasting and fitting F = A + Bv + Cv². C is mostly aerodynamics; A is mostly tyres.
Froad = ½ ρ Cd A v² + Crr m g cos θ + m g sin θ + meff dv/dtmeff ≈ 1.03–1.05 m accounts for rotating wheels, rotor and gears. Pwheel = Froad · v.

Aerodynamic drag comes mainly from the pressure difference between the stagnation region at the front and the separated, low-pressure wake behind the vehicle. Rolling resistance is hysteresis: the tyre deforms at the contact patch and the rubber does not return all of that energy. Its coefficient Crr is typically 0.006–0.010 for passenger tyres and depends on compound, inflation pressure, load and temperature. Grade lifts the vehicle; most of that energy returns downhill through regenerative braking. Inertia accelerates it; part of that energy also returns through regeneration.

Where aerodynamics overtakes the tyres

v* = √( 2 Crr m g / (ρ Cd A) )For Cd 0.20, A 2.3 m², 2,400 kg and Crr 0.008: v* ≈ 26 m/s ≈ 94 km/h ≈ 58 mph.

The coast-down test

On a test track the vehicle is brought to high speed, shifted to neutral and allowed to decelerate. Because meff·dv/dt = −F(v), the measured deceleration gives road load directly. Fitting a quadratic gives the road-load coefficients:

F(v) = A + B v + C v²A ≈ rolling resistance and constant drag (brakes, bearings). B ≈ speed-dependent tyre and driveline losses. C ≈ ½ ρ Cd A plus any speed-squared tyre effects.

These coefficients are loaded onto the chassis dynamometer, so every laboratory drive-cycle test reproduces the road. SAE J1263 and J2263 describe the procedures; J2263 adds on-board anemometry to account for wind. Runs are made in both directions to cancel wind and slope.

Fit a coast-down from noisy data

INTERACTIVE · SYNTHETIC DATA
Measured points (from noisy deceleration)Quadratic fitTrue road load

The "true" vehicle uses the calculator's Cd, A, mass and Crr, plus a small B term. Speed is sampled at 1 Hz; deceleration comes from a central difference, which is why noise matters. A steady headwind w turns C(v + w)² into C·v² + 2Cw·v + Cw², so it adds 2Cw to B and Cw² to A. Averaging runs in both directions cancels the error in B but not the error in A, which is why J2263 measures the wind on board.

WHERE CFD FITS

External aerodynamics CFD predicts the C term; tyre and driveline data feed A and B. When a measured road load disagrees with the model, the split between the coefficients points to the term that needs attention.

CHAPTER 03

Drive cycles and the range label

In brief

  • Official consumption comes from speed-versus-time schedules driven on a dynamometer. The urban (UDDS) and highway (HWFET) schedules below are the actual EPA traces.
  • A backward-facing model follows the trace: speed → wheel force → drivetrain efficiency → battery power, with regeneration when braking.
  • In the city, inertia and regeneration dominate. On the highway, aerodynamics dominates.

The US label combines city and highway results with a 55/45 weighting. Manufacturers can run the two-cycle test and multiply the result by 0.7 to approximate real-world driving, or run the full five-cycle procedure, which adds an aggressive high-speed cycle (US06), a hot cycle with air conditioning (SC03) and a cold-temperature city cycle. Battery electric vehicles follow the SAE J1634 multi-cycle range test. The simulator below is a simplified model of the physics, not the certification procedure.

"Approximate label" applies 55/45 weighting and the 0.7 factor to battery-side energy; the certified value also includes charging losses and follows SAE J1634.

Backward-facing and forward-facing models

A backward-facing model starts from the required speed trace and computes what each component must deliver, working back from the wheels to the battery. It is fast, always follows the cycle and suits efficiency and range studies. A forward-facing model starts from a driver controller that commands torque; components respond with their own dynamics and limits, and the vehicle speed is an output. It is slower but captures controls, saturation and transients, which matters for performance, thermal transients and controller development. Commercial system tools such as GT-SUITE, Simulink/Simscape and Simcenter Amesim support both approaches.

CHAPTER 04

Aerodynamic drag

In brief

  • Range responds to the drag area CdA, not to Cd alone.
  • Pressure drag dominates passenger cars; the rear end and wake are the main levers, followed by wheels, underbody and cooling flow.
  • CFD predicts differences between variants more reliably than absolute drag, unless the test conditions and the wake are well resolved.

A drag count is 0.001 in Cd. On a low-drag sedan at highway speed, aerodynamics is a little more than half of battery energy, so 10 counts (5% of a Cd of 0.20) change consumption by roughly 3%. Use the calculator in chapter 2 to see the value for your own vehicle and speed.

Where the drag comes from

  • Rear end and wake. A tapered roof and sides with a sharp cut-off edge give a smaller, steadier wake and higher base pressure.
  • Wheels and wheelhouses. Rotating, open and turbulent; often a fifth to a third of total drag. Wheel covers, tyre profile, deflectors and air curtains are the levers.
  • Underbody. A flat, closed floor and a rear diffuser. The battery pack of an electric vehicle naturally provides a smooth floor.
  • Cooling flow through the heat exchangers (chapter 5), and protuberances such as mirrors and handles.
  • Ride height. Lowering at speed reduces frontal area and underbody flow.

Yaw and wind-averaged drag

Natural wind combined with driving speed gives a yaw angle of a few degrees, and drag usually rises with yaw. Wind-averaged drag evaluates Cd at several yaw angles and weights them by how often they occur, so a design that only wins at zero yaw does not look better than it is.

CHAPTER 05

Cooling drag and front-end airflow

In brief

  • Air that passes through the heat exchangers loses momentum; that momentum deficit is drag.
  • More cooling air means more heat rejection and more drag. Active shutters let the thermal system take only the air it needs.
  • The cooling demand peaks when ram air is weakest: fast charging and slow, high-load driving.
Dcooling ≈ ṁair (V∞ − Vexit)The momentum lost by the cooling air between the grille and its exit into the underbody or wheel arches.

Electric vehicles need less cooling air at cruise than combustion vehicles, but they still reject heat from the air-conditioning condenser or heat-pump outdoor exchanger, the power electronics and the battery during fast charging. Sealing the front end so that no air bypasses the cores, and guiding the exit flow cleanly, are classic gains. The fan is a pure auxiliary load, often several hundred watts at full speed.

In CFD, each core is usually a porous medium whose resistance is fitted to the measured pressure drop against face velocity, with the fan represented by a pressure jump from its curve or by a rotating zone when swirl matters:

Δp / L = (μ / α) v + C2 · ½ ρ v²Darcy–Forchheimer form: a viscous term and an inertial term.

WHERE CFD FITS

A front-end airflow model gives radiator airflow against vehicle speed, with the fan on and off, as the boundary condition for the 1D thermal model. The same model gives the drag cost of each shutter position, so heat rejection and drag can be traded in one place.

CHAPTER 06

Drive-unit losses

In brief

  • Copper loss grows with torque squared; iron loss grows with speed. Peak efficiency is high, but cruising sits at low torque where iron loss is a large share.
  • Copper resistance rises about 0.39% per kelvin, and permanent magnets lose flux as they heat. Cooling is an efficiency topic as well as a durability one.
  • The number that matters is efficiency weighted over the drive cycle, not the peak.

CHAPTER 07

Battery and voltage

In brief

  • Range uses usable energy; a buffer at both ends of the state of charge protects cell life.
  • Internal loss is I²Rint. Resistance rises sharply in the cold.
  • Doubling pack voltage halves current for the same power and divides resistive losses by four in the same conductors.

Cells prefer a narrow temperature window. Cold cells have high resistance and risk lithium plating while charging; hot cells age faster. Uniformity matters as much as the mean: cells at different temperatures age at different rates, so a cold plate must remove heat evenly, which makes battery cooling a flow-distribution problem.

P = V I  →  Ploss = I² R = (P / V)² R250 kW at 800 V is 312 A; at 400 V it is 625 A and four times the resistive heat in the same conductors.

A dimpled or ribbed cold plate can be reduced the same way as any compact heat-exchanger surface: simulate one periodic cell of the pattern, extract the Fanning friction factor and the Colburn j factor against Reynolds number, and use those correlations in a plate-level or pack-level model. The trade-off is explicit: more heat transfer costs pressure drop, and pressure drop costs pump power (chapter 8).

CHAPTER 08

Pumps, fans and compressors

In brief

  • Hydraulic efficiency from CFD is Phyd/Pshaft. It contains only what the model contains.
  • The battery pays wire-to-water: hydraulic × volumetric × mechanical × motor × controller. Five stages at 90% give 59%.
  • In a closed loop the system curve is H = kQ². Slowing the pump keeps it near its best-efficiency point; half the flow costs about one eighth of the power. Throttling with a valve wastes most of that.
H = Δpt / (ρ g)    Phyd = Q Δpt = ρ g Q H    Pshaft = T ω    ηh = Phyd / Pshaft

In CFD the shaft power comes from the torque on the impeller walls, so the efficiency includes blade and volute losses and, only if they are meshed, disk friction and leakage. Bearings, seals, motor and controller sit outside it.

Head and efficiency curves are least-squares quadratics through the five accepted MRF points of the public pump study (7 blades, 7000 rpm, 900–1650 L/h, ρ = 1074 kg/m³). Speed control uses the affinity laws along the loop's own parabola, so the pump stays at the efficiency of the design point. Throttling keeps 7000 rpm and adds a valve. The motor-and-controller efficiency is an assumption; the CFD efficiency covers the impeller and casing only.

Operating point, viscosity and cavitation

The pump only sees the loop through its system curve, H = kQ², where k sums every restriction: valves, cold plates, chiller, motor jacket, hoses. Removing a restriction lowers k, so the same flow needs less head and less power, or a smaller pump. Cold coolant raises viscosity, which raises k and degrades the pump's own curve. If inlet pressure approaches vapour pressure (hot coolant, restrictive suction, low system pressure), cavitation reduces head and erodes the impeller; suction-side restriction matters more than discharge-side.

Fans and compressors

Radiator fans obey the same affinity laws, so two fans at moderate speed can cost less than one at full speed. The refrigerant compressor is the largest thermal auxiliary load. Its power rises with pressure ratio, so pressure losses on the refrigerant side, in lines, chiller, condenser and valves, raise compressor power and lower the system coefficient of performance.

CHAPTER 09

Heating and winter range

In brief

  • A resistive (PTC) heater has a coefficient of performance of 1. A heat pump moves heat from outside air or waste heat and delivers two to three times its electrical input in mild cold.
  • Heating is paid per hour, so its cost per kilometre is largest in slow winter traffic.
  • Cold also raises battery resistance, tyre rolling resistance, driveline viscosity and air density.
COP = Qheat / Pelectric    Pelectric = Qheat / COPHeat demand ≈ UA (Tcabin − Tambient), with ventilation air included in UA.

Thermal architecture decides how much of the heat is bought with electricity. Waste heat from the drive unit, inverter and battery can be routed to the cabin or the battery instead of being rejected, which requires multi-port coolant valves and mode switching. The battery can be pre-conditioned before fast charging, so it accepts high power sooner.

CHAPTER 10

Fast-charging heat

In brief

  • Charging power tapers as cell voltage approaches its limit; 10–80% time is the usual metric.
  • Heat generation scales with current squared, and the vehicle is standing still: the chiller and the front-end fan carry the entire load.
  • Better heat-exchanger models let a design charge closer to the cell limit without oversizing the cooling.
Qheat ≈ (1 − ηcharge) PchargeA 3% loss at 250 kW is 7.5 kW of heat in cells, busbars, cables and power electronics.

The limits are the charger, the connector current, the cell voltage and temperature. Cold cells risk lithium plating and must be heated first; hot cells are derated to protect life. The chiller, a refrigerant-to-coolant heat exchanger, links the battery loop to the refrigerant cycle, so its pressure drop and conductance enter both the coolant pump and the compressor power.

WHERE CFD FITS

A plate-fin chiller can be reduced in three steps: a periodic fin cell gives friction and Colburn j factors against Reynolds number and flow angle; the correlations are frozen and versioned; the full stack replaces the fins with a porous medium that uses them, with heat sources for the heat transfer. The same f and j curves are what a 1D heat-exchanger model needs.

CHAPTER 11

Which lever is worth more

In brief

  • Express every proposal as Δ energy per distance on a named cycle, then as range, then compare with cost and mass.
  • On the highway, drag counts beat kilograms. In the city, mass, regeneration and auxiliary power gain weight.
  • For small changes, % Δrange ≈ −% Δconsumption ≈ (share of that loss) × (% change of that loss).

Efficiency also compounds through the battery. A heavier vehicle needs more battery for the same range, which adds mass and cost. Energy saved per kilometre can be taken as more range or as a smaller, cheaper and lighter pack, which is why efficiency is often discussed as a cost lever.

CHAPTER 12

From CFD to the system model

In brief

  • A vehicle energy model is only as good as its component inputs. Detailed CFD is one of the best sources for them.
  • The useful deliverable is a reduced form the system model can run: a map, a curve, a correlation or a calibrated component.
  • State the validity range with every reduced model, and correlate the assembled model on the same cycles as the test.

Identify the physics before fitting

Pressure loss in a hydraulic component has a viscous part, proportional to flow and viscosity, and an inertial part, proportional to flow squared. Which part dominates changes with temperature and flow rate, so a reduced model calibrated at one condition can fail badly at another. A calibration that respects which physics controls each parameter, using operating points across the whole envelope, gives a component that stays valid where the system model will use it. The same rule applies to drag maps, heat-exchanger correlations and loss maps: know which mechanism each fitted coefficient represents, and state the range over which it was fitted.
