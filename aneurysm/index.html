01 / MODEL FIDELITY

Choose the model against the quantity that matters.

Geometry, viscosity law and flow-model assumptions interact. The research compared them through defined outputs instead of treating increased model complexity as a guarantee of a better answer.

Start with a controlled comparison

Image-based domain preparation, inlet/outlet definition and near-wall meshing established the computational regions. The model-comparison study imposed pulsatile inflow with a 0.75 s period, then examined constitutive and laminar/k–ε assumptions.

Three different geometries made it possible to ask whether a conclusion was a feature of the chosen model or of the particular flow domain.

The transferable task: make a modeling choice defensible by holding the comparison conditions clear and measuring its effect on an engineering output.

Rheology: how viscosity responds to local shear

A constant Newtonian viscosity is a baseline. Carreau, power-law and Casson descriptions give different low-shear behavior—precisely where recirculation can make a constitutive assumption important.

The result depends on geometry

The thesis reports mean TAWSS differences between Carreau and Newtonian cases of 19%, 16% and 3% for AAA1, AAA2 and IA. The small difference in one geometry cannot justify a universal Newtonian approximation.

The useful conclusion is a sensitivity assessment: decide whether the viscosity model matters for the flow region and output being used.

Connect the physics to a defined metric

TAWSS is cycle-averaged wall-shear magnitude, in Pa. OSI measures shear-direction oscillation. They answer different questions; a similar mean shear level can coexist with a changed directional history.

Extracting, averaging and interpreting these outputs is part of the work—not just selecting a solver option.

Constitutive equations and parameters used in the comparison

Newtonian: μ = 0.00345 Pa·s. Carreau: μ = μ∞ + (μ₀ − μ∞)[1 + (λγ̇)²](n−1)/2, with μ₀ = 0.056 Pa·s, μ∞ = 0.00345 Pa·s, λ = 3.313 s and n = 0.3568.

Power law: μ = Kγ̇n−1, K = 0.009267 Pa·sⁿ and n = 0.828. Casson: μ = [√(τc/γ̇) + √m]², with m = 0.0031 Pa·s and τc = 0.01081 Pa. The displayed 0.1–1000 s⁻¹ interval illustrates these laws; it is not a measured shear-rate range for every case.

Turbulence assessment: scrutinize the assumption

The work compared laminar and standard k–ε descriptions and examined behavior across pulsatile cycles. Later-cycle OSI patterns changed more in the aortic comparison than in the selected intracranial geometry.

This demonstrates turbulence-model assessment and time-dependent interpretation. Recirculation or cycle variation alone does not establish turbulent flow; these were not DNS or LES calculations.

The animation below is a separate, idealized sidewall-sac visualization. Following the vectors helps explain inflow, local rotation and exchange with a recirculating region before reading more abstract wall indices.

Its vectors represent the velocity field, not particle trajectories or proof of turbulence.

02 / FLUID–STRUCTURE INTERACTION

Let fluid loading and structural response interact.

A deformable boundary introduces an additional feedback: fluid forces change the wall, and the moving wall changes the flow domain.

  1. Fluid loadingCalculate pressure and viscous traction at the interface.
  2. Solid responseResolve displacement under the chosen elastic material model.
  3. Moving interfaceTransfer wall motion and update the ALE fluid mesh.
  4. Coupled convergenceIterate the interface response within the time step.

From a boundary condition to a coupled problem

The formulation used a linear isotropic elastic wall and an arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian description for the fluid mesh. The surviving solids4Foam benchmark configuration selects partitioned IQN-ILS coupling.

This requires attention to interface transfer, mesh motion and convergence in addition to the fluid solution itself.

Ask whether compliance changes the conclusion

The selected aortic rigid/elastic comparison reports differences of 23% in diastolic WSS, 19% in OSI and 43% in GON. GON describes temporal cancellation of the wall-shear-gradient vector.

The importance of wall motion therefore depends on the output. Similar broad flow patterns do not establish agreement in oscillatory or gradient-sensitive quantities.

Transfer: coupling fluid loads, structural deformation and mesh motion is the same class of numerical task encountered in compliant flow passages, flexible components and other fluid-loaded structures. Those applications are skill transfers, not additional projects claimed here.

03 / VERIFICATION AND VALIDATION

Test the coupling where the answer is interpretable.

An elastic-tube pressure wave isolates the fluid/solid feedback before complex geometry is introduced. The benchmark connects animation, analytical prediction and numerical sensitivity.

Stiffness changes wave propagation

The thesis benchmark uses a 1333 Pa pulse lasting 0.003 s. The analytical relation combines fluid compressibility and wall compliance, giving a reference for the numerical pressure-wave speed.

The reported maximum analytical/numerical discrepancy is 6%. It is meaningful because the compared quantity, the reference and the tested family are defined.

This verifies a coupled numerical response. It does not validate anatomical material properties or a clinical outcome.

Separate model sensitivity from discretization sensitivity

For the elastic tube, mesh and time-step comparisons examined the pressure profile. The thesis reports differences up to approximately 3% for mesh and 2% for time step. The full plots make the sensitivity visible instead of reducing validation to a badge.

Check the output used in the anatomical comparison

The separate aneurysm meshes were assessed through surface-averaged WSS histories over a pulsatile cycle. Near-wall resolution and the time-dependent output are shown together, linking meshing decisions to the quantity eventually interpreted.

04 / LAGRANGIAN TRANSPORT

Follow what the flow carries, not only what it does at the wall.

Particle tracking adds a different question: how injected material moves through the unsteady field and exchanges with recirculating regions.

Configure the dispersed phase deliberately

A surviving DPMFoam setup specifies one-way Lagrangian tracking, inlet injection, 5 μm particles, sphere drag and rebound at walls. One-way coupling means particles respond to the carrier flow without feeding forces back into it.

Injection, particle properties and wall interaction are physical modeling choices. They determine what the trajectory calculation can represent.

Distinguish trajectory time from wall indices

Age is elapsed time since injection. It is different from RRT, a wall-shear-derived index, and from a residence-time distribution calculated from trajectories.

The work demonstrates dispersed-phase configuration and transient transport visualization. Quantitative trapping or residence statistics require the corresponding trajectory data and run definition.

Transfer: Lagrangian injection, drag and wall-interaction modeling are relevant to dispersed-material transport in internal flows. Particle size, forces and coupling assumptions would be reassessed for each new application.
